> **In short:**
> 1. A **sourdough starter for brioche** must be **young and mild**: two or three feedings within 24 hours, used at its peak.
> 2. Dose: **20 to 30 % of the flour weight** in liquid starter, so **150 g for 500 g**.
> 3. The starter brings flour and water: **take 75 g of flour and 75 g of liquid** out of the original recipe.
> 4. Allow **around twenty hours** in total, including a night in the cold. A pinch of yeast on top secures the rise.

A **sourdough starter for brioche** is not prepared like a starter for a country loaf. Brioche is one of the hardest doughs to raise: 50 % butter, 12 % sugar and a dough moistened almost entirely with eggs. All of this slows down the wild yeasts in the starter. And the sourness that gives a sourdough loaf its charm becomes a fault in a crumb that is expected to be soft and buttery.

So the work happens upstream, on the starter itself, far more than on the dough. The dough follows the same logic as the [old-fashioned brioche recipe](/en/blog/old-fashioned-brioche/): gluten network first, butter second, a night in the cold.

## Liquid starter or stiff starter

A starter is a culture of wild yeasts and lactic bacteria kept alive by feedings, that is, regular additions of flour and water. Two forms exist side by side, distinguished by their hydration.

| | Liquid starter | Stiff starter |
|---|---|---|
| Hydration | 100 %, as much water as flour | around 50 % |
| Texture | pourable, like a thick batter | supple dough that can be kneaded |
| Acidity | mild, lactic | sharper if kept cool |
| Strength | decent | higher |
| Ease | the simplest to manage | needs more attention |

**For a first brioche, a liquid starter is the right choice.** It mixes easily into an already very rich dough, and it mainly produces lactic acid, with yoghurt notes. A stiff starter kept cool develops more acetic acid, which is sharper. Yet it is a stiff starter that makes the great Italian brioches such as panettone, but fed very often and kept warm precisely so it stays mild.

## The feedings that make the starter mild

A starter taken out of the fridge after a week is too acidic and too slow for brioche. It has to be woken up and "washed" of its acidity through close feedings.

1. **First feeding, the morning before**: 1 part starter to 2 of flour and 2 of water, for example 25 g of starter, 50 g of T65 flour and 50 g of lukewarm water.
2. **Leave at 25 to 28 °C** until it doubles, in 4 to 6 hours.
3. **Second feeding**, in the same proportions, starting from 25 g of this starter. The rest is discarded.
4. **A third feeding** if the starter still smelled of vinegar at the second. Each round dilutes the accumulated acidity and strengthens the yeasts.
5. **Use at the peak**, when the starter has doubled or tripled, its surface is domed and covered in bubbles, and it smells of yoghurt or fruit, not vinegar or acetone.

The peak lasts one to two hours. After that, the starter collapses and turns acidic, and it is precisely a collapsed starter that gives a sour brioche.

Temperature matters as much as rhythm. Below 22 °C, the starter sours more than it rises. An oven switched off with the light on, or the top of a fridge, often sits around 26 °C.

## How much goes into the dough

Starter is measured as a percentage of the total flour weight. For a brioche, the useful range is **20 to 30 % of active liquid starter**. Below that, the rise drags on for too long in such a rich dough. Above it, the sourness starts to show.

The point not to forget: **a liquid starter is half flour, half water**. 150 g of starter brings 75 g of flour and 75 g of water, which must come out of the recipe to keep the same dough.

## Converting a yeast recipe

Based on a brioche with 500 g of flour, 6 eggs and 250 g of butter:

| Ingredient | Yeast version | Sourdough version |
|---|---|---|
| Strong white flour or T45 | 500 g | 425 g |
| Active liquid starter | 0 | 150 g |
| Fresh yeast | 10 to 12 g | 0, or 2 to 3 g as insurance |
| Whole eggs | 6, about 300 g | 5, about 250 g |
| Milk | 2 to 3 tablespoons | 0 |
| Unsalted butter | 250 g | 250 g |
| Sugar | 60 g | 60 to 70 g |
| Salt | 10 g | 10 g |

The egg and milk removed roughly offset the 75 g of water in the starter. The sugar can go up slightly to balance the small residual acidity, without exceeding 15 % of the flour weight: beyond that, it slows the starter even more.

**Yeast as insurance is not cheating.** Many bakers combine starter with a small dose of yeast in rich doughs. 2 to 3 g of fresh yeast shorten the final proof by several hours and keep most of the starter's flavour. For a first attempt, it is the option to favour.

It should not be confused with a **yeasted pre-ferment**, made of flour, water and baker's yeast, with no natural starter. It adds some fermentation flavour, but it does not make a sourdough brioche.

## The two-day timetable

The starter slows everything down. Allow **around twenty hours** between the last feeding and taking the brioche out of the oven, which fits over a weekend. Example for a brioche served at Sunday brunch, whose [usual hours](/en/blog/what-is-brunch/) revolve around midday:

| Time | Step | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Saturday 8 am | First feeding | 4 to 6 h |
| Saturday 1 pm | Second feeding | 4 to 6 h |
| Saturday 6 pm | Kneading, butter added last | 30 min |
| Saturday 6:30 pm | Bulk rise at room temperature | 3 to 4 h |
| Saturday 10 pm | Fold, then overnight in the fridge | 9 to 10 h |
| Sunday 7 am | Shaping while cold | 20 min |
| Sunday 7:30 am | Final proof between 26 and 28 °C | 4 to 5 h |
| Sunday 12 pm | Bake at 180 °C | 25 to 30 min |

The final proof is the longest and least predictable step. The dough should have clearly swollen, at least doubled, and spring back slowly when pressed with a finger. **Do not go above 28 °C** to speed it up: beyond that, the butter melts and seeps out of the dough.

With 2 to 3 g of yeast added, the final proof drops to around 2.5 to 3 hours, and the brioche can come out of the oven before 11 am.

## The mistakes that ruin a sourdough brioche

- **Using the starter straight from the fridge.** It is acidic and half asleep: the brioche barely rises and smells sour.
- **Waiting for the starter to collapse.** The peak is the only right moment, and it does not last.
- **Forgetting to remove the starter's flour and water.** The dough becomes sticky and impossible to shape.
- **Proofing too warm.** Above 28 °C, the butter separates from the dough.
- **Baking too early.** An under-proofed sourdough brioche is dense and tight. The proof is judged by volume, not by the clock.

## What the starter changes on the plate

A sourdough brioche does not taste like a sourdough loaf. Done well, the acidity is barely noticeable, in the background. It brings a slightly more melting crumb, a longer flavour and better keeping: the brioche stays soft a day longer than a yeasted one, as the acidity slows staling.

It is not any lighter for all that: the butter and eggs are the same. Once stale, it makes an excellent [traditional French toast](/en/blog/traditional-french-toast/), its slight acidity balancing the sweetness of the custard. And in thick slices on a [breakfast tray](/en/blog/breakfast-tray/), it easily replaces pastries bought the day before.

On cost, 425 g of flour, 250 g of butter and 5 eggs come to about 5 euros for a brioche of over a kilo, while sourdough brioche from artisan bakeries is among the most expensive items in the window. The only real cost is time, and most of it is spent waiting.
